01 — The Crisis

Why poor prehospital care is lethal.

More than half of all deaths come from conditions that could be treated with timely emergency care — trauma, heart attacks, strokes, sepsis, obstetric and pediatric emergencies.

The Golden Hour

Most deaths happen in the first hour — and outside the hospital.

Because time-sensitive conditions turn fatal fast, gaps in prehospital care translate directly into avoidable mortality and disability. The clock starts long before the emergency room does.

15–50%

Trauma death rates before hospital arrival

Systematic reviews show prehospital trauma death rates ranging from about 15% to nearly 50% — and a substantial share are preventable: roughly 5–11% definitely and 26–43% potentially, with faster transport, better on-scene management and fewer errors.

On the front line — every second counts
The Reality Today

Fragmented. Uncoordinated. Often “suboptimal”.

Across most of Asia, emergency transport frequently relies on private cars, taxis, or auto-rickshaws — vehicles with no basic life-support capability — contributing to delays and unsafe handling of critically ill patients.

Studies repeatedly link long response times, missing protocols, minimal triage and out-of-pocket payment barriers to worse outcomes — with higher mortality when the response is slow, unorganized, or clinically weak.

  • A.

    Long response & transport times

    No GPS dispatch, poor road access, and vehicles placed without regard to incidence hotspots.

  • B.

    No standardized protocols

    Care depends on who shows up — often bystanders with minimal training and no clinical pathway.

  • C.

    Minimal triage capability

    Under-triage, over-triage and wrong-hospital decisions increase mortality and ED crowding.

  • D.

    Out-of-pocket payment barriers

    Cost deters poor patients from calling an ambulance at all — the deadliest delay of them all.

Treated as a last resort, the ambulance stays scarce — and that scarcity silently becomes loss.
